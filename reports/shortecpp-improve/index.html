shortECPP revisited: prover changes, ECM engine benchmark, and a model of the search

2026-11-14 · branch shortecpp-improve · follows the review of the "Short ECPP prover: recommended improvements" note. Hardware: one c8a.48xlarge spot box (AMD EPYC 9R45, Zen 5, 192 cores, no SMT) and the 16-core driver box, which was shared with another job all day, so local timings are CPU times and may be up to twice the idle figures.

Summary.
  • Prover. Supply and round handling are reworked and tested: the scan's factor base is capped from the first scan and its square roots are cached, the strip uses a 226 prime product, classification no longer runs Miller–Rabin in Python on every prime tail, and a round stops at its first winner. At 1030 bits a scan to 6·1010 (99,338 discriminants) costs 4.5 core-hours; in August the scan to 2·1010 (85,954) cost about 210.
  • Engine. Re-tuning GMP and gmp-ecm for Zen 5 gains at most 3% with all cores busy. avx-ecm's stage 1 is 1.8 times faster than gmp-ecm's (1.35–1.5 times per whole curve), but only in its non-IFMA build: the IFMA build of the standalone repository computes wrong residues.
  • Model. A model of one level with no fitted parameter reproduces the 1030-bit campaign log tier by tier. It says the schedule, not the engine, is where the time goes: a per-candidate stopping rule that gives most orders no ECM and takes the best few much deeper costs several times less per winner than fixed tiers with admission caps.
  • Experiment. That rule, run with real gmp-ecm on 249,648 orders of nextprime(2768), found 17 winners for 26.9 core-hours of ECM (1.6 per winner) plus about 10 core-hours of supply. The model had predicted 24.2 winners for 31.8 core-hours. On the same prime and box, the prover with its old scheduler needed 59 core-hours of search to reach its first winner; the August campaign's 765-bit prime took 148.
  • Construction. With winners this cheap, building the curve costs more than finding it, and at 768 bits 61–99.6% of a build is root-finding on the full-degree class polynomial (section 6). The class-group decomposition of arXiv:1009.1082 removes most of that and is not implemented in this tree.
  • Not done. The prover's scheduler still uses the old tiers and caps; putting the stopping rule into it, and the decomposition into cm_method, are the next two steps. yafu's IFMA ECM was not built.

1. What changed in the prover

All of this is in ecpp/shortECPP.py, ecpp/dscan.c and ecpp/smoothtest.c. The scheduler (tiers, admission caps, ranking, widen rule) is unchanged: these are the changes that needed no model.

The modified prover produced verified revised-format certificates for nextprime(10100), nextprime(10120) and nextprime(10160) on the driver box and, on the spot box, for nextprime(2768) (section 5).

2. Where supply stands now

With the cap, the scan costs about 0.1 core-seconds per solvable discriminant at B = 2·1010 and 0.25 at the margin near 6·1010: the count of solvable D grows like √B while tests grow like B, so supply is cheap but its price rises with the pool. The count is flat in the cap: on the 1030-bit dump, a cap of B/4000 keeps 81% of what B/400 finds, with a ninth of the roots.

3. ECM engine benchmark

Built on the box: GMP 6.3.0 twice (as configured by default, and with --build=zen3-pc-linux-gnu), gmp-ecm 7.0.6 against each, a third gmp-ecm with its own make ecm-params tuning, Ubuntu's gmp-ecm 7.0.5 package (the campaign binary), and bbuhrow/avx-ecm (commit 118e015, March 2021). Inputs are products of two primes of half the size, so every curve runs to the end.

3.1 Re-tuning gmp-ecm is not worth doing

GMP 6.3's configure does not recognise Zen 5 and silently selects the generic x86_64/k8 code; forcing the Zen 3 code paths gives 2% on a 17-limb multiplication and 12% on a squaring. On an otherwise idle box the Zen build with tuned gmp-ecm parameters is 7% faster in stage 1 and 12% in stage 2 at 1030 bits. With every core busy, which is how the prover runs, the difference is 3% or nothing:

3.2 avx-ecm: stage 1 is 1.8 times faster, but only its non-IFMA build is correct

The standalone avx-ecm repository has two AVX-512 code paths. The IFMA path (built with ICELAKE=1) is wrong on this machine at every optimisation level I tried: it never found a 10-digit factor that the other path finds at once, and none of its saved stage-1 residues agreed with an independent computation modulo a known factor (data/chk_avx.py: 0 of 3; the non-IFMA build: 6 of 6). The repository's own to-do list still says "IFMA and benchmarking". The non-IFMA build (SKYLAKEX=1) is correct, and gmp-ecm's -resume accepts its residues and finds factors in stage 2.

3.3 The shallow tier is not launch-bound

24,000 calls of ecm -q -c 12 2000 on 1030-bit inputs through a 192-thread Python pool ran at 716 calls a second and 93% utilisation; eight tails per process and a process pool made no difference (92% and 94%). So one interpreter can launch far more than the 335 calls a second seen in the campaign's 1030-bit round, where utilisation was about 44%. The difference in the campaign is unexplained (the prover passes a timeout, which puts communicate() on a slower path, and its heap is much larger); the tier is 2% of the run either way.

4. A model of the search

ecpp/shortsim.py. After the strip, an order's tail T wins exactly when T = (primes ECM removes) · q with q a prime in the candidate's coverage: the set of q for which some divisor m of the n²-smooth part s gives L < mq < r(m)L. On the real 1030-bit orders the coverage measures log2 s bits almost exactly (mean 21.4 bits; 18.0 under the revised format's limits). The model has three ingredients and no fitted parameter:

  1. Prime factors of T between the strip bound and 2160 form a Poisson process with intensity db/b in the bit size b; what is left after them is a 2160-rough number, prime with the density Buchstab's function gives.
  2. A prime of b bits survives c curves at a tier with probability (1 − π(b))c, with π measured for gmp-ecm on the box (section 8, data/pi_calib.json) where the measurement has at least 8 successes, and otherwise the two-stage Dickman function for orders near 2b/24, rescaled to join the measured cells.
  3. Sums of prime sizes are then compound-Poisson laws, computed exactly on a 1-bit grid by FFT. This gives, for a tail of t bits known to be composite after j tiers, the probability of every outcome of tier j+1: nothing found, a smaller composite tail, or a prime tail (a win if it lies in the coverage).

4.2 What it says about the schedule

If fresh orders can always be bought at a fixed price, the best policy is a stopping rule per candidate: run the next sweep on a composite tail exactly when its expected value is positive. The model computes it by dynamic programming over (sweep, tail size) for each coverage class, with the value of a win found by bisection. 8.8 core-s per order is what supply cost in the August 1030-bit run; 1.0 and 0.3 bracket what it costs now (scan 0.1–0.25, strip under 0.01, plus overheads). In brackets: orders bought per winner and ECM spent per order.

Two things produce the gain, and both go against a ladder with fixed admission caps:

  • Selection. The win probability is proportional to the coverage and falls by about 3% per bit of tail above √p, so a candidate with a 100-bit smooth part and 50 bits of coverage is worth tens of typical ones. With cheap supply the best policy gives most orders no ECM at all (at 1.0 core-s per order, 29% of fresh orders get the B1 = 2,000 sweep, 11% reach 11,000, 2.8% reach 50,000 and 0.6% reach 250,000), where the reference runs B1 = 50,000 on 45% of the pool and 250,000 on 15%.
  • Depth. On the few candidates that survive, it keeps going well past where the reference stops; in the third row of the table 37% of the winners come from the 106 sweeps and 58% from 3·106. This is the same effect as the campaign logs show directly: 11 of the 15 top-level winners on the 192-core boxes came from the B1 = 106 tier in 2.6 hours of wall time, 3 from 250,000 in 6.2 hours.

5. Experiment: the policy on real orders at 768 bits

The model's stopping rule was computed for the real orders of p = nextprime(2768) (scan to 2·1011 with the cap at 108, revised-format coverage, supply priced at 0.3 core-seconds per order, sweeps up to B1 = 3·107 on offer) and then executed with gmp-ecm 7.0.5 on the 192-core box by data/policy_run.py: each composite tail runs the next sweep exactly when the table says so for its coverage class and current size, a tail that becomes prime is tested against the format's window, and nothing else is consulted. The prediction in the fifth column used the theoretical π, because the measurement of π finished after the run. 17 winners against 24.2 is about 1.5 standard deviations low for a Poisson count. Re-predicted with the measured π, the same policy is expected to find 20.9 winners, within one standard deviation of 17. The model still sends 15–20% more tails to the deep sweeps than actually arrived, so some optimism is real, but it is right about the structure: 14 of the 17 winners came from the 106 and 3·106 sweeps on a few hundred tails, and the policy never asked for B1 ≥ 107 at this size.

Cost of a top-level winner at 768 bits, all in: 9.1 core-hours of scan and under 1 of strip for the whole pool, 26.9 of ECM, so about 2.2 core-hours per winner; the run took 759 seconds of wall time, about a fifth of it single-threaded Python in my driver. For comparison:

  • The August campaign's 765-bit prime (10230+753) took 148 core-hours and 83,506 orders for its one winner; 698 and 731 bits took 183 and 41.
  • The model's expectation for the reference scheduler's order of work on these same orders, at today's supply cost, is 33–37 core-hours of ECM before the first winner: it spends 25–60 core-hours on B1 ≤ 250,000 across the pool before it reaches the sweeps where the winners are.
  • Run straight after the experiment for comparison: the modified prover with its old scheduler, from scratch on the same prime and box, reached its first winner after 1,110 s (59 core-hours, 53.5 of them ECM; 63,182 orders) and completed a verified chain in 1,224 s. It spent 43 core-hours on B1 ≤ 50,000 across the pool before the second 250,000 sweep found the winner, and the new early stop ended that round after 2.8 s. This is one draw, and somewhat worse than the model's expectation.

The winners have |D| between 5.2·109 and 1.7·1011: with a pool this wide, winners are spread over the whole scan range and curve construction, not the search, becomes the expensive part of a level. A complete revised-format certificate for nextprime(2768) was built on the box from the winner with the smallest |D|, D = −5,201,738,755: the chain took 112 s, 107 of them the top-level curve; it passes vshort2.verify and the PARI cross-check, and the baseline run above, which found the same winner, produced the identical certificate (data/p768_cert.txt).

6. Curve construction is now the bottleneck

A wide pool spreads winners over the whole scan range, so their discriminants are large: the 17 winners of section 5 have class numbers from 11,364 to 489,056. cm_method on four of them, alone on the 192-core box, p = nextprime(2768):

Three things follow.

  • Construction now costs more than the search. The cheapest build, 110 s on the box, is 5.9 core-hours of box time (1.8 of actual CPU); a winner costs about 2.2 to find.
  • It is almost all root-finding. With a good class invariant the polynomial itself arrives in seconds from parallel classpoly; the root of the full-degree polynomial by equal-degree splitting takes 61–99.6% of the time, and keeps only 40–65 of the 192 cores busy.
  • The invariant matters as much as h. The fourth discriminant only admits invariant 26 and its polynomial takes a hundred times longer than the others.

This is the case for the decomposition of Sutherland, Accelerating the CM method: with a subgroup of order n and m = h/n cosets, one finds a root of a polynomial V of degree m, forms from it a polynomial of degree n, and finds a root of that, so the degrees in the root-finding drop from h to m and n. How much that buys depends on the divisors of h. For the first row h = 22·3·947, so the best split is degrees 947 and 12 in place of 11,364; for the third, 266 and 220 in place of 58,520. Of the 17 winners, 12 have a split with both degrees at most 600, and with winners this cheap one can choose among them. The vendored classpoly_v1.0.3 already has the consuming half for word-size primes (ff_classpoly_root, the F/G file format, tower_split), but nothing in the tree produces a decomposed polynomial, and cm_method always takes the full-degree route.

7. What I would do next

  1. Put the stopping rule into prove_level_cm. Replace the caps, the index and the widen rule by a table lookup (coverage class, sweep, tail bits) computed once per level by shortsim.py, feed orders as a stream priced at the scan's marginal cost, and spread the curves of a deep sweep over idle cores when few tails are left. Validate on nextprime(2768) against today's numbers, then at 1030 bits, where the model expects 30–50 core-hours per top-level winner against 330–410 for the reference.
  2. Root-finding through the class group (section 6). Implement the two-level decomposition for the big prime: a decomposed output mode in the CRT code and a cm_method path that finds a root of V with fproot, evaluates the degree-n polynomial at it and finds a root of that. Then the paper's second algorithm if the CRT data for the interpolants becomes the limit. The 17 journalled winners (data/exp768_winners.jsonl) are ready-made test cases.
  3. Price construction into the stopping rule. A winner's value should be net of its build cost, which depends on h, on the invariant available and, after the previous item, on the shape of the class group. Until the decomposition exists it pays to wait for a winner with a small class number.
  4. Stage 1. Either wire the non-IFMA avx-ecm in as a stage-1 engine with gmp-ecm -resume for stage 2 (1.35–1.5 times per curve, measured), or build yafu's IFMA ECM and repeat section 3.2 on it (up to 2 times if it is correct). Both need the 208-bit rounding and the start-up sieve handled at the shallow tiers.
  5. Tighten the model. Measure π and the cost per curve at B1 = 107 and 3·107, which the 1030-bit policy uses and which are extrapolated here; find where the 15–25% optimism comes from (the run counts at the deep sweeps are the place to look); extend it below 640 bits, where the rough-cofactor approximation stops being valid, for the lower levels.
  6. For the paper. The model is a heuristic analysis of the search in closed form (the cost per winner is the fixed point of the stopping problem), with a direct experimental check.
