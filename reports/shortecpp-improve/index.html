shortECPP revisited: prover changes, ECM engine benchmark, and a model of the search

2026-11-14, sections 9 to 11 added 2026-11-15 · branch shortecpp-improve · follows the review of the "Short ECPP prover: recommended improvements" note. Hardware: one c8a.48xlarge spot box (AMD EPYC 9R45, Zen 5, 192 cores, no SMT) and the 16-core driver box, which was shared with another job all day, so local timings are CPU times and may be up to twice the idle figures.

Summary.
  • Prover. Supply and round handling are reworked and tested: the scan's factor base is capped from the first scan and its square roots are cached, the strip uses a 226 prime product, classification no longer runs Miller–Rabin in Python on every prime tail, and a round stops at its first winner. At 1030 bits a scan to 6·1010 (99,338 discriminants) costs 4.5 core-hours; in August the scan to 2·1010 (85,954) cost about 210.
  • Engine. Re-tuning GMP and gmp-ecm for Zen 5 gains at most 3% with all cores busy. avx-ecm's stage 1 is 1.8 times faster than gmp-ecm's (1.35–1.5 times per whole curve), but only in its non-IFMA build: the IFMA build of the standalone repository computes wrong residues.
  • Model. A model of one level with no fitted parameter reproduces the 1030-bit campaign log tier by tier. It says the schedule, not the engine, is where the time goes: a per-candidate stopping rule that gives most orders no ECM and takes the best few much deeper costs several times less per winner than fixed tiers with admission caps.
  • Experiment. That rule, run with real gmp-ecm on 249,648 orders of nextprime(2768), found 17 winners for 26.9 core-hours of ECM (1.6 per winner) plus about 10 core-hours of supply. The model had predicted 24.2 winners for 31.8 core-hours. On the same prime and box, the prover with its old scheduler needed 59 core-hours of search to reach its first winner; the August campaign's 765-bit prime took 148.
  • Construction. With winners this cheap, building the curve cost more than finding it, almost all of it root-finding on the full-degree class polynomial (section 6). The class-group decomposition of arXiv:1009.1082 (Algorithm 1) is now implemented in classpoly and cm_method (section 7): the 17 winners at 768 bits build in a median of 11.7 s each on 192 cores, and the four timed before take 145 s and 2.6 core-hours in total where they took 2,072 s and 49.
  • yafu's ECM. The ECM in current yafu, built with AVX-512 IFMA, is correct and costs 4 to 5 times less per curve than gmp-ecm on 1030-bit inputs with all cores busy, at the same B2 and with at least the same success rate (section 8). It is not yet used by the prover.
  • Scheduler (section 9, 15 November). The stopping rule is in the prover. Thirteen fresh 768-bit primes were proved on the 16-core driver box in 202 s on average (median 112 s), with 1.5 thread-hours of search for the top level; the old scheduler needed 59 and 148 core-hours of search on the 192-core box for two primes of this size. The model's win rate holds up: the wins it expected from the work done, at the first win, average 0.66 over 12 runs, where an exact model gives 1 ± 0.29. The 1030-bit prime 10310 + 733 took 1.41 hours on the same 16 cores (43.9 thread-hours); the August campaign spent about 812 core-hours on it.
  • On 192 cores (section 10, 15 November). 26 primes of 768 bits take 44 s each on average (median 36 s) and 1.56 core-hours, three of 1030 bits 671 s on average and 27.2 core-hours; the campaign prime 10310 + 733 took 167 s. Search cost and wall time agree with the simulation, and over the 26 primes the wins the model expected at the first win average 0.86, where an exact model gives 1 ± 0.20.
  • yafu as the ECM engine (section 11, 15 November). The prover runs yafu with engine=yafu. Its success per curve was measured as finely as gmp-ecm's (6 million curves): at equal B1 a yafu curve succeeds as often or up to 30% more often, and costs 3.3 to 5.2 times less. On the 16-core driver box the thirteen 768-bit primes take 41 s each on average (median 34 s) where gmp-ecm took 202 (112), and 10310 + 733 takes 60 minutes on a slow draw where gmp-ecm took 85 on a fast one. In simulation a level on 192 cores costs 0.77 core-hours instead of 1.81 at 768 bits and 12.6 instead of 41.3 at 1030 bits.
  • The table of ShortPrimalityProofs (section 12, 16 and 17 November). On one 192-core box with yafu, every entry from 1010 to 10400 is proved faster than recorded, by a factor 2.5 to 748 in CPU time; the seventeen entries from 10160 to 10320 take 17 minutes and 42 core-hours in all, where the table records 4,180 core-hours, and the eight from 10330 to 10400 take 6.0 hours and 1,019 core-hours, where it records 51 hours and 8,290. The runs exposed three defects, now repaired: yafu abandons a number at its first factor, levels below 500 bits ran with the parameters of large ones, and a missing modular polynomial cost a minute of retries. The stopping rule now runs from 280 bits.
  • A new entry: nextprime(10410) = 10410 + 67 (section 12.7). 1362 bits, five levels, 7.8 hours on 192 cores and 953 core-hours: 4.8 hours of search and 3.0 of construction, nearly all of that for one root of a polynomial of prime degree 470,279. A first run stalled, because this prime has a quarter of the usual number of small discriminants; the scan now gets its orders from a wider bound or from a higher cap on the primes of a discriminant, whichever is cheaper.
  • Not done. A build still spends most of its time in root-finding at the prime part of the class number: with yafu it is a third of a 768-bit level on 192 cores, and it was three of the eight hours of 10410. Its cost is predictable from the class number; the scheduler uses that when a winner is in hand, not yet when a candidate comes in. The least primes above 10420, 10430, 10440 and 10450 are running as this is written (section 12.9).

3. ECM engine benchmark

Built on the box: GMP 6.3.0 twice (as configured by default, and with --build=zen3-pc-linux-gnu), gmp-ecm 7.0.6 against each, a third gmp-ecm with its own make ecm-params tuning, Ubuntu's gmp-ecm 7.0.5 package (the campaign binary), and bbuhrow/avx-ecm (commit 118e015, March 2021). Inputs are products of two primes of half the size, so every curve runs to the end.

3.1 Re-tuning gmp-ecm is not worth doing

GMP 6.3's configure does not recognise Zen 5 and silently selects the generic x86_64/k8 code; forcing the Zen 3 code paths gives 2% on a 17-limb multiplication and 12% on a squaring. On an otherwise idle box the Zen build with tuned gmp-ecm parameters is 7% faster in stage 1 and 12% in stage 2 at 1030 bits. 3.3 The shallow tier is not launch-bound

24,000 calls of ecm -q -c 12 2000 on 1030-bit inputs through a 192-thread Python pool ran at 716 calls a second and 93% utilisation; eight tails per process and a process pool made no difference (92% and 94%). So one interpreter can launch far more than the 335 calls a second seen in the campaign's 1030-bit round, where utilisation was about 44%. The difference in the campaign is unexplained (the prover passes a timeout, which puts communicate() on a slower path, and its heap is much larger); the tier is 2% of the run either way.

4. A model of the search

ecpp/shortsim.py. After the strip, an order's tail T wins exactly when T = (primes ECM removes) · q with q a prime in the candidate's coverage: the set of q for which some divisor m of the n²-smooth part s gives L < mq < r(m)L. On the real 1030-bit orders the coverage measures log2 s bits almost exactly (mean 21.4 bits; 18.0 under the revised format's limits). The model has three ingredients and no fitted parameter:

  1. Prime factors of T between the strip bound and 2160 form a Poisson process with intensity db/b in the bit size b; what is left after them is a 2160-rough number, prime with the density Buchstab's function gives.
  2. A prime of b bits survives c curves at a tier with probability (1 − π(b))c, with π measured for gmp-ecm on the box (section 8, data/pi_calib.json) where the measurement has at least 8 successes, and otherwise the two-stage Dickman function for orders near 2b/24, rescaled to join the measured cells.
  3. Sums of prime sizes are then compound-Poisson laws, computed exactly on a 1-bit grid by FFT. This gives, for a tail of t bits known to be composite after j tiers, the probability of every outcome of tier j+1: nothing found, a smaller composite tail, or a prime tail (a win if it lies in the coverage).

4.2 What it says about the schedule

If fresh orders can always be bought at a fixed price, the best policy is a stopping rule per candidate: run the next sweep on a composite tail exactly when its expected value is positive. The model computes it by dynamic programming over (sweep, tail size) for each coverage class, with the value of a win found by bisection. Two things produce the gain, and both go against a ladder with fixed admission caps:

  • Selection. The win probability is proportional to the coverage and falls by about 3% per bit of tail above √p, so a candidate with a 100-bit smooth part and 50 bits of coverage is worth tens of typical ones. With cheap supply the best policy gives most orders no ECM at all (at 1.0 core-s per order, 30% of fresh orders get the B1 = 2,000 sweep, 11% reach 11,000, 2.7% reach 50,000 and 0.6% reach 250,000), where the reference runs B1 = 50,000 on 45% of the pool and 250,000 on 15%.
  • Depth. On the few candidates that survive, it keeps going well past where the reference stops; in the third row of the table 36% of the winners come from the 106 sweeps and 57% from 3·106. This is the same effect as the campaign logs show directly: 11 of the 15 top-level winners on the 192-core boxes came from the B1 = 106 tier in 2.6 hours of wall time, 3 from 250,000 in 6.2 hours.

Three things follow.

  • Construction now costs more than the search. The cheapest build, 110 s on the box, is 5.9 core-hours of box time (1.8 of actual CPU); a winner costs about 2.2 to find.
  • It is almost all root-finding. With a good class invariant the polynomial itself arrives in seconds from parallel classpoly; the root of the full-degree polynomial by equal-degree splitting takes 61–99.6% of the time, and keeps only 40–65 of the 192 cores busy.
  • The invariant matters as much as h. The fourth discriminant only admits invariant 26 and its polynomial takes a hundred times longer than the others.

This is the case for the decomposition of Sutherland, Accelerating the CM method: with a subgroup of order n and m = h/n cosets, one finds a root of a polynomial V of degree m, forms from it a polynomial of degree n, and finds a root of that, so the degrees in the root-finding drop from h to m and n. How much that buys depends on the divisors of h. For the first row h = 22·3·947, so the best split is degrees 947 and 12 in place of 11,364; for the third, 266 and 220 in place of 58,520. Of the 17 winners, 12 have a split with both degrees at most 600, and with winners this cheap one can choose among them. The vendored classpoly_v1.0.3 already has the consuming half for word-size primes (ff_classpoly_root, the F/G file format, tower_split), but at the time of these measurements nothing in the tree produced a decomposed polynomial, and cm_method always took the full-degree route. Section 7 describes the implementation that followed.

7. The class-group decomposition, implemented

For a subgroup G of the class group with n elements and m cosets, classpoly now computes, in place of the class polynomial H, a polynomial V of degree m whose roots are the m coset values yi (minus the sum of the roots in the coset) and n−1 interpolants Wk with m coefficients each. cm_method finds a root y of V, forms U(X) = Xn + yXn−1 + Σ (Wk(y)/V′(y)) Xk, which is a factor of H mod p, and finds a root of U. Two root-findings of degrees m and n replace one of degree mn, and V and the Wk have smaller coefficients than H, so the CRT uses fewer primes. The output is in the F/G file format that the vendored classpoly already reads for word-size primes. It is requested with CLASSPOLY_TOWER=1; cm_method uses it by default (tower=0 turns it off).

What had to be added to the paper's algorithm, and what it turned up:

  • A wider family of subgroups. The enumeration of the roots mod p does not control the direction in which it walks each generator, so only some subgroups have orbits that can be read off from positions. The paper's implementation uses G = ⟨a1, …, ad−1, ade⟩. That is not enough here: for D = −5,201,738,755 the presentation has relative orders (2, 1894, 3), and the split 12 × 947 is not of that form. I use G = ⟨aici⟩ with ci dividing the relative order ni, under the condition that whenever ci < ni the power relation aini = ∏j<i ajrij has cj dividing rij. Then G has order ∏ ni/ci, the positions with all residues ei mod ci fixed lie in one coset and are as many as G has elements, so they are a coset, whatever the directions. This relies on each generator being walked in one direction throughout, which the gcd steps of the enumeration ensure. The argument is in the comments of compute_classpoly.c; it is mine, not the paper's. (15 November: confirmed as sound; section 9.5 adds a second family for the subgroups this one misses.)
  • No trace computation for the Weber functions. For an invariant f whose class polynomial is only determined mod p up to f → −f (Weber f, and the eta quotients that are square roots), classpoly first computes the trace of the class polynomial by a CRT of its own. With 192 workers each of them repeats it: at h = 117,668 that was 23 to 58 s per worker before the main loop began. The decomposition has a way round it. Each coefficient of V and of the Wk has a parity under f → −f, and the trace T is odd; storing every odd coefficient multiplied by T, together with T2, makes all stored numbers independent of the sign. The consumer divides by a square root of T2 mod p and gets the decomposition of H(X) or of H(−X), which give the same j-invariants. For D = −24,483,261,047 this took the build from 97 s and 14,700 core-seconds to the figures in the table. (CLASSPOLY_TOWER=2; the file then carries a line S= and is not a plain F/G file.)
  • The height bound is heuristic, and it can be too small. For j the bound of the paper is rigorous. For other invariants the code scales the bounds on |j| by the height factor, as the paper suggests, and that is right on average over the class group but not coset by coset. Over Z, for D = −18,960,475,695 with w5,7 ramified, the true height with 16 cosets is 71,992 bits against a bound of 69,890 (3.0% over); with 8 cosets it is 65,463 against 66,412. A decomposition computed with too few primes is wrong, and nothing in the CRT says so. The defences are: 5% added to the bound for invariants other than j; every failure on the decomposition path (V or U not splitting, V′(y) = 0, no candidate j with the right trace) triggers one retry with 20% added and then the plain class polynomial; and a polynomial that does not split is now given up after one failed split rather than thirty. 1 of the 17 builds needed the retry. A proper bound for the eta quotients, from the sizes of the four eta values involved, would remove the guesswork.
  • Root-finding is what remains. It is 230 s of the 359 s above. Below degree 4096 it was single-threaded; the first splitting step now runs with about lg(degree) random shifts at once, one per core, and intersects the results, which brought the root-finding of these 17 builds from 316 s to 230 s. Above degree 4096 fproot multiplies in parallel, and that gains little: a root of degree 4682 took 60 s with 47 cores busy, where the single-threaded rate at lower degrees predicts 68. The two slowest builds are those whose class number has a large prime factor (9337 and 3571), where no balanced split exists.
  • Choosing the subgroup. classpoly lists the admissible subgroups, computes the height bound of each from the table of reduced forms, and takes the one with the least estimated wall time for CRT plus the two root-findings, from three constants measured on this box. Balanced splits win when the CRT is cheap; a split with few cosets, which has the smallest height, wins when it is not. For these 17 discriminants the height bound of the chosen decomposition is 1.5 to 5.0 times smaller than the plain one (median 2.45); 8 of them use the sign-free variant and 4 are square roots (data/tower_candidates.log).
  • Checks. 908 decompositions over Z, with j and more than 20 other invariants, with square roots of class polynomials and with subgroups in and outside the paper's form, were compared with the plain class polynomial in PARI: modulo three splitting primes each, V has m distinct roots and the product of the U equals H. All agree. Among 3,494 decompositions of discriminants below 2,000 one has a repeated coset value (D = −1603 with A71), which classpoly detects, reverting to the plain polynomial. ecpp/test_tower.py runs a selection of these, the explicit-CRT and multi-job paths, the sign-free variant, and the retry and fallback paths with deliberately damaged files.
  • Found on the way. The plain height bound is about twice the true height whenever the level is ramified and the square root of the class polynomial is computed (median 0.47 over 23 discriminants), so that path does twice the CRT work it needs; I have not changed it. qform_table_free left a dangling pointer that crashed a second call of compute_classpoly, and fproot's schoolbook remainder mishandled a dividend of lower degree than the divisor; both are fixed.

9. The stopping rule in the prover

Levels of 500 bits and more are now run by a new scheduler, ecpp/shortsched.py; smaller levels, and everything with sched=old, keep the tiered rounds. The level is treated as a bandit problem: every order is an arm, a pull is a few ECM curves, fresh arms can be bought from the scan, and the level ends at the first win.

9.1 The rule

  • State of a candidate. Coverage class, sweeps done, tail size in bits, as in section 4. The class needs no divisor enumeration: the divisors m of the smooth part s cover every δ in (0, log2 s), so the class is log2 s rounded. (Take the primes of s in descending order: the powers of the largest tile (0, e1 log p1), and each further prime extends every tile boundary to the right by its own power. The profiles of the 249,648 orders of section 5 are exact initial segments.)
  • Break-even reward r. For every state, the least value of a win, in core-seconds, at which the next sweep pays under the best continuation. It comes from the dynamic programme of section 4 solved on a grid of rewards, 2 to 6 s per level (shortsim.IndexTable), and it does not depend on what orders cost. Its reciprocal is the state's index, so work is done in increasing order of r.
  • The stopping rule. From the same table and the orders seen so far comes the value of a fresh order as a function of the reward; the reward R at which that value equals the marginal price of the next scan chunk is what a win is worth. A candidate is worked only while r ≤ R. R rises as the scan widens, and a candidate parked earlier comes back when R reaches it.
  • Supply. The scan runs in chunks that double B, on the share of the cores that its price has in the cost of a fresh order, and a chunk starts when the work below R is about to run out. Nothing is assumed about the price: it is measured chunk by chunk.
  • Many cores. A sweep is cut into units of a few seconds, and the sweeps of one B1 may be in flight together when their r allows it; a larger B1 waits for the smaller ones to report. Without this the eventual winner's deep sweeps run one after the other, for minutes, while every other core works through candidates that the sequential rule would not have touched.
  • Costs. gmp-ecm's cost per curve is calibrated at the start of a run (two curves at B1 = 50,000 on every thread at once). In the runs below, measured against modelled cost is within about 10% from B1 = 11,000 to 106 and 15% high at B1 = 2,000 (process launches) when the box is otherwise idle, and within 25% in the runs that shared it with simulations.

9.5 What the runs turned up on the build side

  • A second family of subgroups for the decomposition. The boxes of section 7 miss subgroups when a generator's order exceeds its relative order. Run 6: relative orders (2, 2, 1061, 2) with a31061 = a2; the subgroup of order 1061 is generated by a32 and is no box, so the build used 4 × 2122 where 8 × 1061 exists. What the enumeration needs is that the cosets can be read off from positions whatever direction each generator was walked in. That holds when the lattice LG of exponent vectors lying in G is mapped to itself by every change of sign of a coordinate, and those lattices are exactly {x : xi = diyi, y mod 2 ∈ S} for a vector d dividing the columns of the relation matrix and a subspace S of F2k containing the reduced relation rows. The coset of a position is its residues mod d together with the class of (e div d) mod 2 modulo S. Neither family contains the other (the comment in compute_classpoly.c has an example each way), so both are enumerated. Over Z, 532 lattice decompositions on 78 discriminants agree with the plain class polynomial in PARI (data/tower_sweep_lattice.log). Of 29 real winners the choice changes for one, run 6: 26.4 s → 12.2 s.
  • Hits that cannot work on the HD curve. If an odd prime ℓ divides both the cofactor m and v, then ℓ divides π − 1 in the maximal order, the curve has full ℓ-torsion, and no cofactor multiple has exact order o. build_level tried 80 points on each such hit before going to the volcano floor; it now goes there directly. Run 4: 14.8 s → 2.9 s.
  • Restart on a wrong curve. If the curves with the computed j have neither N nor the twist's number of points, which a too-small height bound that slipped past cm_method's checks would cause, the prover now recomputes j from the plain class polynomial instead of dropping the winner. It has not fired.
  • What is left in a build. Root-finding at the prime part of the class number: 11 s at degree 1061 and 23 s at degree 2011 on this box, almost all of it the 768 modular squarings of one powering, each three multiplications of integers of 1.5 to 3 megabits in GMP.

10.3 Is the model's win rate right?

At a level's first win the wins the model expected from the work done should be exponentially distributed with mean 1. Over the 26 primes here the mean is 0.86, where an exact model gives 1 ± 0.20 (the thirteen primes of section 9.3 give 0.65, the thirteen new ones 1.07), and the largest distance between the empirical distribution and the exponential one is 0.22 (Kolmogorov–Smirnov, 5% point 0.27). So the model's overall win rate is right to within what 26 primes can show.

Two things qualify that.

  • Runs on the same prime are not independent draws. The thirteen primes of 9.3 have now been proved three times: on the driver box, here, and with yafu in section 11. For 9 of them the same discriminant won at least twice, for 3 all three times, and the three means of the expected wins are 0.65, 0.65 and 0.62. The first winner is largely a property of the prime's pool of orders, not of the random curves, so repeating a prime adds little, and the 0.65 of section 9.3 is those thirteen primes.
  • Easy winners. 8 of the 26 primes won before the model expected 0.12 wins, where the exponential law gives 2.9, and four won after it expected more than 1.8, where the law gives 4.3. The early ones are orders whose medium primes are all within reach of the first sweeps; the same order then wins on every machine. The model may underrate these, which would make the rule a little too patient with the rest. It is the one place where the data pull away from the model, and with 26 primes it is a hint, not a finding.

11.3 In the prover

  • Batches of 8. The scheduler has a curve quantum: every unit is a multiple of 8 curves and the ladder is B1 = 2,000 × 16, 11,000 × 24, then sweeps of 16 curves (2, 3, 5, 6, 6 and 4 of them at 50,000 to 3·107). Sweeps of 8 instead of 16 make no difference in simulation.
  • Engine profile. Cost table, growth with size, stage-2 bounds and success table are per engine (shortsim.ENGINES); the break-even table is built from the engine's own numbers, and the cost per curve is calibrated at the start of a run as for gmp-ecm.
  • Calling yafu. One process per unit, expressions ecm(n, curves) on standard input, -avxecm_stg2 1 (B2 depends on B1 only), logs off, and -aprcl_p 1: by default yafu proves a prime cofactor prime with APR-CL, two seconds at 1000 bits, every time a tail turns out to be prime. Its curves are seeded from the clock, the process id and the system's random source, so processes running at once use different curves. The parts it reports are checked to multiply back to the input.
  • Self-test. A yafu binary is used only if it finds a planted factor at start-up; otherwise the prover says so and runs gmp-ecm.

12.2 What the first pass showed

  • yafu stops at the first factor. ecm(n, curves) drops an input after the batch of 8 curves in which a factor appears, whatever is left of the curves asked for and even if the cofactor is composite (gmp-ecm goes on with the cofactor). The scheduler was crediting whole sweeps. It matters most at the first sweep, where most tails lose a small prime: at 299 bits the first sweep delivered 3 winners where the model expected 14. Crediting the curves actually run and offering the rest again is not enough: the model's state "j sweeps done, tail of t bits" means a tail that survived those sweeps in full, and a tail that has just lost a 25-bit prime is not a fresh tail of its new size. The runner now runs the remaining curves on the cofactor in a further process, until the sweep is complete.
  • Levels below 500 bits. They were run by the old tiered rounds with the parameters of large levels: in four of the 32 chains of the first pass the second level cost more than the first (up to 140 s at 449 bits). Section 12.3.
  • Missing modular polynomials. The repository carries Φℓ for ℓ ≤ 71 and the spot box had only those. A discriminant whose class group needs a larger ℓ (here Φ101 for the Weber function) made classpoly abort without a message, and the prover retried it six times, ten seconds each with 64 jobs, before going back to the search: four chains of 32 lost one to three minutes that way. The message is now printed whatever the verbosity, the prover gives up on such a discriminant at once and names the file, and the later passes ran with the full set (2.2 GB). Since 17 November the repository carries the Weber polynomials (f, f2) and eight double eta families with small files up to ℓ = 109: of 3,000 random fundamental discriminants between 107 and 1010, 99 asked for a missing file before and 31 do now, 20 of them for an Atkin function.
  • The rule table. Its dynamic programme ran in threads and gained nothing from them (thousands of short numpy calls: the threads hand the interpreter lock around). It took 4 s per level, half of a chain at 10160 to 10200. In worker processes it takes 0.6 s, with identical tables. The workers are child interpreters fed over pipes; a fork server re-imports the caller's main script, and one of my check scripts reran itself eight times that way.

12.3 Small levels

A level's first scan, strip bound and thread count now follow its size (level_params): a scan to 105 and a 216 strip on one thread below 200 bits, up to the old 3·107, 226 and all threads from 500. The stopping rule runs from 280 bits. For that the model's bound D between medium primes and the rough cofactor follows the size (a third of it, 160 bits from 485 up), the ladder stops where its curves would reach D (B1 ≤ 50,000 below 320 bits), the reward grid scales with the size, and a shared ECM process stops growing at 0.05 expected wins: at 300 bits the best few candidates are each a fair chance, and what runs alongside the unit that wins is wasted.

The step is tested on simulated levels and in unit tests; no run on 192 cores has exercised it yet.

12.5 The scan's share of the cores

On 192 cores with yafu, 40 to 100% of the ECM core-seconds of a level are handed out "above the rule", to cores that have no work below it while a scan chunk is running. I tried giving the scan enough cores for its chunk to arrive as the work below the rule runs out. In simulation (768 bits, 192 threads) that costs 0.78 core-hours and 17.4 s per level against 0.65 and 13.7 s: a faster scan buys the next chunk, twice as dear, sooner, while the work above the rule is mostly work brought forward, since the rule's reward rises with every chunk. The change is not in.

12.7 nextprime(10410) = 10410 + 67

The table ends at 10400. The certificate is certs/short2/nextprime1e410.csv: 1362 bits, five levels, 28,153 s on 192 cores, 953 core-hours, checked by vshort2.py and PARI.

A first run stalled. After three hours it had 356,128 orders, 0.52 expected wins, 2,829 candidates through the whole ladder, and a scan at 8.2·1012, close to its bound of 1013, whose last chunk had cost 5.7 core-seconds an order; I stopped it. This prime has 5,692 solvable discriminants below 109 where 10400 + 69 has 20,888: the supply price differs between primes of one size by more than the model's win rate does.

Two ways to more orders. The scan has two bounds, B on |D| and the cap on the prime factors of D (the factor base). Only B used to move, the cap following as B/1000 and stopping at 108. But a discriminant that was passed over for a prime above the cap is as good as any other of its size, and there are many: at B = 4·1012 a cap of 108 admits a little over half of the discriminants. RuleDriver.next_chunk now prices both moves from what the scans have measured and takes the cheaper per order. Widening takes B to 2B at the present cap: orders grow like √B, work like B. Raising takes the cap to four times its value over the range already scanned: the orders gained are the orders in hand times the gain in the density of cap-smooth discriminants, at the measured cost per discriminant tested plus one square root modulo p for every prime added, and the prediction is corrected by the outcome of the last raise. This was Drew's suggestion. dscan does a raise without repeating anything: with Bmin = B and fbmin at the old cap it lists only the products that contain a new prime, and skips the prefixes of old primes that leave no room for one (test_dscan.py checks that an earlier scan and a raise together are the full scan at the new cap). The cap's ceiling is a quarter of the machine's memory for the square roots, 2·1010 on this box at 1362 bits. At 1000 bits the price per order is what it was (data/yafu_c8a_6). For this prime the cap went up six times, from 106 to 4.1·109, each time the predicted price of a raise was below that of a wider bound (by 3 to 20%). At B = 4.1·1012 the scan had 327,326 orders with its cap at 109 where the first run, with its cap at 108, had 263,290. The whole supply of 362,752 orders cost 1.03 million core-seconds where the first run paid 1.16 million for 356,128, that is 13% less per order, and the next orders were priced at 5.9 core-seconds each where the first run's next doubling would have cost about 7.7.

The run. Search 4.8 hours: 362,752 orders, 286 core-hours of scan and 430 of ECM, 0.73 expected wins at the win, which came from a sweep at B1 = 3·107. Levels of 1300 bits and more now have 192 curves at that B1 on offer where they had 64. B1 = 108 was timed and left out: 4.4 times the cost of 3·107 for about twice the success on factors of 120 to 160 bits. Construction 3.0 hours: the winner is D = −1,400,983,335,911 with class number 4 × 470,279; the decomposed class polynomial took 1,490 s of CRT, and the root of its factor of prime degree 470,279 took 9,376 s on about 20 of the 192 cores, where the law of 12.4 said 13,137 s. The reserve step of 12.4 let that build go ahead, rightly: another winner was an expected six hours away. The step has still not held a winner back in any real run (25 runs of 768-bit primes drew no costly one).

12.8 yafu's success at the deep sweeps

The first calibration (11.1) has 768 curves a cell at B1 = 107 and 3·107 and few hits beyond 110 bits. A second one adds 598,344 curves on factors of 102 to 174 bits at B1 = 3·106, 107 and 3·107 (data/yafu_pi_calib_deep.py): 445, 694 and 526 hits where the table then in use expected 512, 885 and 555, that is 0.87, 0.78 and 0.95 of them. The reason is in how the table was read: cells with 8 hits or more were kept, and the ratio to theory of the last one was carried to every larger size. That selects on the outcome, since the last cell to qualify is one that came out high: beyond 110 bits the rate in use was 1.44 times theory at B1 = 5·104 and 1.49 times at 2.5·105. The two calibrations are now pooled (data/fold_yafu_calib.py), and a cell's ratio to theory is pooled with its neighbours until the pool holds 100 hits, cells without a hit included. Read this way, yafu's success from 100 bits up is 0.88 to 1.13 times the two-stage Dickman curve with gain 24 at the three deep values of B1, and 0.94 and 1.11 times at 5·104 and 2.5·105. The gmp-ecm table is read the same way; its largest change is at B1 = 106 beyond 110 bits, where one cell of 14 hits between two of 8 and 7 had set the rate at twice what the pool gives. Sections 3 to 11 of this page are computed with the tables as now read.

12.9 Checkpoints, and four larger primes

A level of 1400 bits is a search of many hours on a machine that can be taken away at two minutes' notice, and until now only the winners in hand survived that. Levels of 900 bits and more write their search state every ten minutes: the scan's two bounds and its measurements, the loop's counters, and every live candidate with its tail and its place on the ladder, about 150 bytes each. resume=1 takes the search up from it; what is lost is the units in flight and the factor base's square roots, which the next scan computes again. Tested by killing a 768-bit level after its third checkpoint and resuming it (the queue by B1 continued where it had stopped), and in simulation (test_sched.py). A build is not checkpointed: a winner survives, its class polynomial and root start again.

Since 18 November, 00:23 UTC, the least primes above 10420, 10430, 10440 and 10450 are running, one per 192-core box: 10450 + 2269 (1495 bits) and 10440 + 709 (1462 bits) on c8a.48xlarge, 10430 + 1587 (1429 bits) and 10420 + 1261 (1396 bits) on c7a.48xlarge (Zen 4), which is what had capacity. Their logs and checkpoints are copied off the boxes as they run. None has finished as this is written; this paragraph will have the outcome.

13. What I would do next

  1. Count the build in a candidate's value (section 12.4). The build time of a winner is known from its class number within a factor 1.25, and it is a third of a 768-bit level on 192 cores, with a tail of one to five minutes. Keeping a costly winner in reserve is in but unmeasured; the 768-bit set on a 192-core box would measure it in five minutes. The fuller form: charge each candidate its build at intake (class numbers of a whole chunk take seconds), so that the rule works a candidate while its break-even reward plus the machine time of its build is below R; the value of a fresh order then has to count the builds of the orders too.
  2. Beyond 10410 (section 12.9). Four primes of 1396 to 1495 bits are running. From 10330 to 10410 the search work per expected win grows about 1.4-fold for every ten digits, and the supply of a prime moves it by a factor of two either way.
  3. Root-finding at prime degree (sections 9.5, 10.2 and 12.4). A powering mod a polynomial of degree 1,000 to 20,000 over a 768- or 1030-bit field is that many squarings as the field has bits, each done as three GMP multiplications of integers of 1 to 40 megabits; at degree 19,583 and 1030 bits it takes 290 s on 192 cores, of which about 50 are busy; at degree 470,279 and 1362 bits it took 2.6 hours on about 20 cores (section 12.7), a third of that proof. Multiplying residue by residue modulo twenty-odd word-size primes with a number-theoretic transform should be several times faster on one core and uses as many cores as there are primes.
  4. Sharper height estimates for the decomposition (section 9.6). The bound is a median 23% too large for invariants other than j. Numerical values of the invariant at the forms would give the heights to a few bits; the plain bound in the ramified case is also about twice too large.
  5. Small things. yafu takes 11 to 27 ms to start, which is most of a unit at 300 bits; the measured ECM cost there is 1.3 to 2 times the model's. For gmp-ecm, π and the cost per curve at B1 = 107 and 3·107 are still extrapolated; for yafu they are measured. The gmp-ecm table, now read with pooled cells (section 12.8), has not been checked against real runs since. The README needs the new arguments and the size-dependent defaults.
  6. For the paper. The search is a bandit problem with a closed-form index: the cost per level is the fixed point of a stopping problem, the rule is an index policy, and both have a direct experimental check.
