    Three things follow.

    • Construction now costs more than the search. The cheapest build, 110 s on the box, is 5.9 core-hours of box time (1.8 of actual CPU); a winner costs about 2.2 to find.
    • It is almost all root-finding. With a good class invariant the polynomial itself arrives in seconds from parallel classpoly; the root of the full-degree polynomial by equal-degree splitting takes 61–99.6% of the time, and keeps only 40–65 of the 192 cores busy.

    This is the case for the decomposition of Sutherland, Accelerating the CM method: with a subgroup of order n and m = h/n cosets, one finds a root of a polynomial V of degree m, forms from it a polynomial of degree n, and finds a root of that, so the degrees in the root-finding drop from h to m and n. How much that buys depends on the divisors of h. For the first row h = 22·3·947, so the best split is degrees 947 and 12 in place of 11,364; for the third, 266 and 220 in place of 58,520. Of the 17 winners, 12 have a split with both degrees at most 600, and with winners this cheap one can choose among them. The vendored classpoly_v1.0.3 already has the consuming half for word-size primes (ff_classpoly_root, the F/G file format, tower_split), but at the time of these measurements nothing in the tree produced a decomposed polynomial, and cm_method always took the full-degree route. Section 7 describes the implementation that followed.

    #### TOWER

    For a subgroup G of the class group with n elements and m cosets, classpoly now computes, in place of the class polynomial H, a polynomial V of degree m whose roots are the m coset values yi (minus the sum of the roots in the coset) and n−1 interpolants Wk with m coefficients each. cm_method finds a root y of V, forms U(X) = Xn + yXn−1 + Σ (Wk(y)/V′(y)) Xk, which is a factor of H mod p, and finds a root of U. Two root-findings of degrees m and n replace one of degree mn, and V and the Wk have smaller coefficients than H, so the CRT uses fewer primes. The output is in the F/G file format that the vendored classpoly already reads for word-size primes. It is requested with CLASSPOLY_TOWER=1; cm_method uses it by default (tower=0 turns it off).

    What had to be added to the paper's algorithm, and what it turned up:

    • A wider family of subgroups. The enumeration of the roots mod p does not control the direction in which it walks each generator, so only some subgroups have orbits that can be read off from positions. The paper's implementation uses G = ⟨a1, …, ad−1, ade⟩. That is not enough here: for D = −5,201,738,755 the presentation has relative orders (2, 1894, 3), and the split 12 × 947 is not of that form. I use G = ⟨aici⟩ with ci dividing the relative order ni, under the condition that whenever ci < ni the power relation aini = ∏j<i ajrij has cj dividing rij. Then G has order ∏ ni/ci, the positions with all residues ei mod ci fixed lie in one coset and are as many as G has elements, so they are a coset, whatever the directions. This relies on each generator being walked in one direction throughout, which the gcd steps of the enumeration ensure. The argument is in the comments of compute_classpoly.c; it is mine, not the paper's. (15 November: confirmed as sound; section 9.5 adds a second family for the subgroups this one misses.)
    • No trace computation for the Weber functions. For an invariant f whose class polynomial is only determined mod p up to f → −f (Weber f, and the eta quotients that are square roots), classpoly first computes the trace of the class polynomial by a CRT of its own. With 192 workers each of them repeats it: at h = 117,668 that was 23 to 58 s per worker before the main loop began. The decomposition has a way round it. Each coefficient of V and of the Wk has a parity under f → −f, and the trace T is odd; storing every odd coefficient multiplied by T, together with T2, makes all stored numbers independent of the sign. The consumer divides by a square root of T2 mod p and gets the decomposition of H(X) or of H(−X), which give the same j-invariants. For D = −24,483,261,047 this took the build from 97 s and 14,700 core-seconds to the figures in the table. (CLASSPOLY_TOWER=2; the file then carries a line S= and is not a plain F/G file.)
    • The height bound is heuristic, and it can be too small. For j the bound of the paper is rigorous. For other invariants the code scales the bounds on |j| by the height factor, as the paper suggests, and that is right on average over the class group but not coset by coset. Over Z, for D = −18,960,475,695 with w5,7 ramified, the true height with 16 cosets is 71,992 bits against a bound of 69,890 (3.0% over); with 8 cosets it is 65,463 against 66,412. A decomposition computed with too few primes is wrong, and nothing in the CRT says so. The defences are: 5% added to the bound for invariants other than j; every failure on the decomposition path (V or U not splitting, V′(y) = 0, no candidate j with the right trace) triggers one retry with 20% added and then the plain class polynomial; and a polynomial that does not split is now given up after one failed split rather than thirty. @@TOWER_RETRIES@@ of the @@TOWER_N@@ builds needed the retry. A proper bound for the eta quotients, from the sizes of the four eta values involved, would remove the guesswork.
    • Root-finding is what remains. It is @@TOWER_ROOT_PAR@@ s of the @@TOWER_WALL_TOTAL@@ s above. Below degree 4096 it was single-threaded; the first splitting step now runs with about lg(degree) random shifts at once, one per core, and intersects the results, which brought the root-finding of these @@TOWER_N@@ builds from @@TOWER_ROOT_SERIAL@@ s to @@TOWER_ROOT_PAR@@ s. Above degree 4096 fproot multiplies in parallel, and that gains little: a root of degree 4682 took 60 s with 47 cores busy, where the single-threaded rate at lower degrees predicts 68. The two slowest builds are those whose class number has a large prime factor (9337 and 3571), where no balanced split exists.
    • Choosing the subgroup. classpoly lists the admissible subgroups, computes the height bound of each from the table of reduced forms, and takes the one with the least estimated wall time for CRT plus the two root-findings, from three constants measured on this box. Balanced splits win when the CRT is cheap; a split with few cosets, which has the smallest height, wins when it is not.     #### RULE

    Levels of 500 bits and more are now run by a new scheduler, ecpp/shortsched.py; smaller levels, and everything with sched=old, keep the tiered rounds. The level is treated as a bandit problem: every order is an arm, a pull is a few ECM curves, fresh arms can be bought from the scan, and the level ends at the first win.

    9.1 The rule

    • State of a candidate. Coverage class, sweeps done, tail size in bits, as in section 4. The class needs no divisor enumeration: the divisors m of the smooth part s cover every δ in (0, log2 s), so the class is log2 s rounded. (Take the primes of s in descending order: the powers of the largest tile (0, e1 log p1), and each further prime extends every tile boundary to the right by its own power. The profiles of the 249,648 orders of section 5 are exact initial segments.)
    • Break-even reward r. For every state, the least value of a win, in core-seconds, at which the next sweep pays under the best continuation. It comes from the dynamic programme of section 4 solved on a grid of rewards, 2 to 6 s per level (shortsim.IndexTable), and it does not depend on what orders cost. Its reciprocal is the state's index, so work is done in increasing order of r.
    • The stopping rule. From the same table and the orders seen so far comes the value of a fresh order as a function of the reward; the reward R at which that value equals the marginal price of the next scan chunk is what a win is worth. A candidate is worked only while r ≤ R. R rises as the scan widens, and a candidate parked earlier comes back when R reaches it.
    • Supply. The scan runs in chunks that double B, on the share of the cores that its price has in the cost of a fresh order, and a chunk starts when the work below R is about to run out. Nothing is assumed about the price: it is measured chunk by chunk.
    • Many cores. A sweep is cut into units of a few seconds, and the sweeps of one B1 may be in flight together when their r allows it; a larger B1 waits for the smaller ones to report. Without this the eventual winner's deep sweeps run one after the other, for minutes, while every other core works through candidates that the sequential rule would not have touched.
    • Costs. gmp-ecm's cost per curve is calibrated at the start of a run (two curves at B1 = 50,000 on every thread at once). In the runs below, measured against modelled cost is within about 10% from B1 = 11,000 to 106 and 15% high at B1 = 2,000 (process launches) when the box is otherwise idle, and within 25% in the runs that shared it with simulations.

    9.5 What the runs turned up on the build side

    • A second family of subgroups for the decomposition. The boxes of section 7 miss subgroups when a generator's order exceeds its relative order. Run 6: relative orders (2, 2, 1061, 2) with a31061 = a2; the subgroup of order 1061 is generated by a32 and is no box, so the build used 4 × 2122 where 8 × 1061 exists. What the enumeration needs is that the cosets can be read off from positions whatever direction each generator was walked in. That holds when the lattice LG of exponent vectors lying in G is mapped to itself by every change of sign of a coordinate, and those lattices are exactly {x : xi = diyi, y mod 2 ∈ S} for a vector d dividing the columns of the relation matrix and a subspace S of F2k containing the reduced relation rows. The coset of a position is its residues mod d together with the class of (e div d) mod 2 modulo S. Neither family contains the other (the comment in compute_classpoly.c has an example each way), so both are enumerated. Over Z, 532 lattice decompositions on 78 discriminants agree with the plain class polynomial in PARI (data/tower_sweep_lattice.log). Of 29 real winners the choice changes for one, run 6: 26.4 s → 12.2 s.
    • Hits that cannot work on the HD curve. If an odd prime ℓ divides both the cofactor m and v, then ℓ divides π − 1 in the maximal order, the curve has full ℓ-torsion, and no cofactor multiple has exact order o. build_level tried 80 points on each such hit before going to the volcano floor; it now goes there directly. Run 4: 14.8 s → 2.9 s.
    • Restart on a wrong curve. If the curves with the computed j have neither N nor the twist's number of points, which a too-small height bound that slipped past cm_method's checks would cause, the prover now recomputes j from the plain class polynomial instead of dropping the winner. It has not fired.
    • What is left in a build. Root-finding at the prime part of the class number: 11 s at degree 1061 and 23 s at degree 2011 on this box, almost all of it the 768 modular squarings of one powering, each three multiplications of integers of 1.5 to 3 megabits in GMP.

    9.6 Height bounds

    The bound for invariants other than j stays heuristic, with the checks downstream and the restart as the safety net. Stopping the CRT when lifted coefficients stop changing does not fit the explicit CRT mod p as it stands: its weights depend on the whole set of primes, so stopping early means starting again.     12.2 What the first pass showed

    • yafu stops at the first factor. ecm(n, curves) drops an input after the batch of 8 curves in which a factor appears, whatever is left of the curves asked for and even if the cofactor is composite (gmp-ecm goes on with the cofactor). The scheduler was crediting whole sweeps. It matters most at the first sweep, where most tails lose a small prime: at 299 bits the first sweep delivered 3 winners where the model expected 14. Crediting the curves actually run and offering the rest again is not enough: the model's state "j sweeps done, tail of t bits" means a tail that survived those sweeps in full, and a tail that has just lost a 25-bit prime is not a fresh tail of its new size. The runner now runs the remaining curves on the cofactor in a further process, until the sweep is complete.
    • Levels below 500 bits. They were run by the old tiered rounds with the parameters of large levels: in four of the 32 chains of the first pass the second level cost more than the first (up to 140 s at 449 bits). Section 12.3.
    • Missing modular polynomials. The repository carries Φℓ for ℓ ≤ 71 and the spot box had only those. A discriminant whose class group needs a larger ℓ (here Φ101 for the Weber function) made classpoly abort without a message, and the prover retried it six times, ten seconds each with 64 jobs, before going back to the search: four chains of 32 lost one to three minutes that way. The message is now printed whatever the verbosity, the prover gives up on such a discriminant at once and names the file, and the later passes ran with the full set (2.2 GB). Since 17 November the repository carries the Weber polynomials (f, f2) and eight double eta families with small files up to ℓ = 109: of 3,000 random fundamental discriminants between 107 and 1010, 99 asked for a missing file before and 31 do now, 20 of them for an Atkin function.

It took 4 s per level, half of a chain at 10160 to 10200. In worker processes it takes 0.6 s, with identical tables.     12.9 Checkpoints, and four larger primes

    A level of 1400 bits is a search of many hours on a machine that can be taken away at two minutes' notice, and until now only the winners in hand survived that. Levels of 900 bits and more write their search state every ten minutes: the scan's two bounds and its measurements, the loop's counters, and every live candidate with its tail and its place on the ladder, about 150 bytes each. resume=1 takes the search up from it; what is lost is the units in flight and the factor base's square roots, which the next scan computes again. Tested by killing a 768-bit level after its third checkpoint and resuming it (the queue by B1 continued where it had stopped), and in simulation (test_sched.py). A build is not checkpointed: a winner survives, its class polynomial and root start again.
