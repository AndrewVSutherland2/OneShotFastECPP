#### SUMMARY
  • Prover. Supply and round handling are reworked and tested: the scan's factor base is capped from the first scan and its square roots are cached, the strip uses a 226 prime product, classification no longer runs Miller–Rabin in Python on every prime tail, and a round stops at its first winner. At 1030 bits a scan to 6·1010 (99,338 discriminants) costs 4.5 core-hours; in August the scan to 2·1010 (85,954) cost about 210.
  • Engine. Re-tuning GMP and gmp-ecm for Zen 5 gains at most 3% with all cores busy. avx-ecm's stage 1 is 1.8 times faster than gmp-ecm's (1.35–1.5 times per whole curve), but only in its non-IFMA build: the IFMA build of the standalone repository computes wrong residues.
  • Model. A model of one level with no fitted parameter reproduces the 1030-bit campaign log tier by tier. It says the schedule, not the engine, is where the time goes: a per-candidate stopping rule that gives most orders no ECM and takes the best few much deeper costs several times less per winner than fixed tiers with admission caps.
  • Experiment. That rule, run with real gmp-ecm on @@EXP_N@@ orders of nextprime(2768), found @@EXP_WINS@@ winners for @@EXP_COREH@@ core-hours of ECM (@@EXP_PER_WIN@@ per winner) plus about 10 core-hours of supply. The model had predicted @@EXP_MODEL_WINS@@ winners for @@EXP_MODEL_COREH@@ core-hours. @@BASELINE_SUMMARY@@
  • Construction. With winners this cheap, building the curve cost more than finding it, almost all of it root-finding on the full-degree class polynomial (section 6). The class-group decomposition of arXiv:1009.1082 (Algorithm 1) is now implemented in classpoly and cm_method (section 7): the @@TOWER_N@@ winners at 768 bits build in a median of @@TOWER_WALL_MEDIAN@@ s each on 192 cores, and the four timed before take @@TOWER_AFTER_WALL@@ s and @@TOWER_AFTER_COREH@@ core-hours in total where they took @@TOWER_BEFORE_WALL@@ s and @@TOWER_BEFORE_COREH@@.
  • yafu's ECM. The ECM in current yafu, built with AVX-512 IFMA, is correct and costs 4 to 5 times less per curve than gmp-ecm on 1030-bit inputs with all cores busy, at the same B2 and with at least the same success rate (section 8). It is not yet used by the prover.
  • Not done. The prover's scheduler still uses the old tiers and caps; putting the stopping rule into it is the next step, and yafu as the ECM engine the one after.

    #### CONSTRUCTION

    A wide pool spreads winners over the whole scan range, so their discriminants are large: the 17 winners of section 5 have class numbers from 11,364 to 489,056. cm_method on four of them, alone on the 192-core box, p = nextprime(2768):

    Three things follow.

    • Construction now costs more than the search. The cheapest build, 110 s on the box, is 5.9 core-hours of box time (1.8 of actual CPU); a winner costs about 2.2 to find.
    • It is almost all root-finding. With a good class invariant the polynomial itself arrives in seconds from parallel classpoly; the root of the full-degree polynomial by equal-degree splitting takes 61–99.6% of the time, and keeps only 40–65 of the 192 cores busy.
    • The invariant matters as much as h. The fourth discriminant only admits invariant 26 and its polynomial takes a hundred times longer than the others.

    This is the case for the decomposition of Sutherland, Accelerating the CM method: with a subgroup of order n and m = h/n cosets, one finds a root of a polynomial V of degree m, forms from it a polynomial of degree n, and finds a root of that, so the degrees in the root-finding drop from h to m and n. How much that buys depends on the divisors of h. For the first row h = 22·3·947, so the best split is degrees 947 and 12 in place of 11,364; for the third, 266 and 220 in place of 58,520. Of the 17 winners, 12 have a split with both degrees at most 600, and with winners this cheap one can choose among them. The vendored classpoly_v1.0.3 already has the consuming half for word-size primes (ff_classpoly_root, the F/G file format, tower_split), but at the time of these measurements nothing in the tree produced a decomposed polynomial, and cm_method always took the full-degree route. Section 7 describes the implementation that followed.

    #### TOWER

    For a subgroup G of the class group with n elements and m cosets, classpoly now computes, in place of the class polynomial H, a polynomial V of degree m whose roots are the m coset values yi (minus the sum of the roots in the coset) and n−1 interpolants Wk with m coefficients each. cm_method finds a root y of V, forms U(X) = Xn + yXn−1 + Σ (Wk(y)/V′(y)) Xk, which is a factor of H mod p, and finds a root of U. Two root-findings of degrees m and n replace one of degree mn, and V and the Wk have smaller coefficients than H, so the CRT uses fewer primes. The output is in the F/G file format that the vendored classpoly already reads for word-size primes. It is requested with CLASSPOLY_TOWER=1; cm_method uses it by default (tower=0 turns it off).

    What had to be added to the paper's algorithm, and what it turned up:

    • A wider family of subgroups. The enumeration of the roots mod p does not control the direction in which it walks each generator, so only some subgroups have orbits that can be read off from positions. The paper's implementation uses G = ⟨a1, …, ad−1, ade⟩. That is not enough here: for D = −5,201,738,755 the presentation has relative orders (2, 1894, 3), and the split 12 × 947 is not of that form. I use G = ⟨aici⟩ with ci dividing the relative order ni, under the condition that whenever ci < ni the power relation aini = ∏j<i ajrij has cj dividing rij. Then G has order ∏ ni/ci, the positions with all residues ei mod ci fixed lie in one coset and are as many as G has elements, so they are a coset, whatever the directions. This relies on each generator being walked in one direction throughout, which the gcd steps of the enumeration ensure. The argument is in the comments of compute_classpoly.c; it is mine, not the paper's, and deserves a second pair of eyes.
    • No trace computation for the Weber functions. For an invariant f whose class polynomial is only determined mod p up to f → −f (Weber f, and the eta quotients that are square roots), classpoly first computes the trace of the class polynomial by a CRT of its own. With 192 workers each of them repeats it: at h = 117,668 that was 23 to 58 s per worker before the main loop began. The decomposition has a way round it. Each coefficient of V and of the Wk has a parity under f → −f, and the trace T is odd; storing every odd coefficient multiplied by T, together with T2, makes all stored numbers independent of the sign. The consumer divides by a square root of T2 mod p and gets the decomposition of H(X) or of H(−X), which give the same j-invariants. For D = −24,483,261,047 this took the build from 97 s and 14,700 core-seconds to the figures in the table. (CLASSPOLY_TOWER=2; the file then carries a line S= and is not a plain F/G file.)
    • The height bound is heuristic, and it can be too small. For j the bound of the paper is rigorous. For other invariants the code scales the bounds on |j| by the height factor, as the paper suggests, and that is right on average over the class group but not coset by coset. Over Z, for D = −18,960,475,695 with w5,7 ramified, the true height with 16 cosets is 71,992 bits against a bound of 69,890 (3.0% over); with 8 cosets it is 65,463 against 66,412. A decomposition computed with too few primes is wrong, and nothing in the CRT says so. The defences are: 5% added to the bound for invariants other than j; every failure on the decomposition path (V or U not splitting, V′(y) = 0, no candidate j with the right trace) triggers one retry with 20% added and then the plain class polynomial; and a polynomial that does not split is now given up after one failed split rather than thirty. @@TOWER_RETRIES@@ of the @@TOWER_N@@ builds needed the retry. A proper bound for the eta quotients, from the sizes of the four eta values involved, would remove the guesswork.
    • Root-finding is what remains. It is @@TOWER_ROOT_PAR@@ s of the @@TOWER_WALL_TOTAL@@ s above. Below degree 4096 it was single-threaded; the first splitting step now runs with about lg(degree) random shifts at once, one per core, and intersects the results, which brought the root-finding of these @@TOWER_N@@ builds from @@TOWER_ROOT_SERIAL@@ s to @@TOWER_ROOT_PAR@@ s. Above degree 4096 fproot multiplies in parallel, and that gains little: a root of degree 4682 took 60 s with 47 cores busy, where the single-threaded rate at lower degrees predicts 68. The two slowest builds are those whose class number has a large prime factor (9337 and 3571), where no balanced split exists.
    • Choosing the subgroup. classpoly lists the admissible subgroups, computes the height bound of each from the table of reduced forms, and takes the one with the least estimated wall time for CRT plus the two root-findings, from three constants measured on this box. Balanced splits win when the CRT is cheap; a split with few cosets, which has the smallest height, wins when it is not. For these @@TOWER_N@@ discriminants the height bound of the chosen decomposition is 1.5 to 5.0 times smaller than the plain one (median 2.45); 8 of them use the sign-free variant and 4 are square roots (data/tower_candidates.log).
    • Checks. 908 decompositions over Z, with j and more than 20 other invariants, with square roots of class polynomials and with subgroups in and outside the paper's form, were compared with the plain class polynomial in PARI: modulo three splitting primes each, V has m distinct roots and the product of the U equals H. All agree. Among 3,494 decompositions of discriminants below 2,000 one has a repeated coset value (D = −1603 with A71), which classpoly detects, reverting to the plain polynomial. ecpp/test_tower.py runs a selection of these, the explicit-CRT and multi-job paths, the sign-free variant, and the retry and fallback paths with deliberately damaged files.
    • Found on the way. The plain height bound is about twice the true height whenever the level is ramified and the square root of the class polynomial is computed (median 0.47 over 23 discriminants), so that path does twice the CRT work it needs; I have not changed it. qform_table_free left a dangling pointer that crashed a second call of compute_classpoly, and fproot's schoolbook remainder mishandled a dividend of lower degree than the divisor; both are fixed.
    #### YAFU

    yafu 3.1.9 (commit 4d88b0b) built with USE_AVX512IFMA=1 on the Zen 5 box. Its ECM is not the standalone avx-ecm of section 3, whose IFMA build computes wrong residues: it uses twisted Edwards curves and an FFT stage 2. It was run the way the prover runs gmp-ecm: one single-threaded process per core with all 192 cores busy, 1030-bit inputs, and here 8 curves per process, which is its vector width. Stage 2 was set to gmp-ecm's B2 (-avxecm_stg2 1) so that a curve means the same thing for both. Times include process start-up.

    #### NEXT
  • Put the stopping rule into prove_level_cm. Replace the caps, the index and the widen rule by a table lookup (coverage class, sweep, tail bits) computed once per level by shortsim.py, feed orders as a stream priced at the scan's marginal cost, and spread the curves of a deep sweep over idle cores when few tails are left. Validate on nextprime(2768) against today's numbers, then at 1030 bits, where the model expects 30–50 core-hours per top-level winner against 330–410 for the reference.
  • yafu as the ECM engine (section 8). Measure its per-curve success by factor size as in section 4, wire it into the prover in batches of 8 curves, and recompute the stopping rule with its costs: a 4 to 5 times cheaper curve moves every threshold.
  • Price construction into the stopping rule. A winner's value should be net of its build cost. After section 7 that cost depends on how the class group splits rather than on h: a winner whose class number has a large prime factor still costs a minute or two. The subgroup choice and its estimate are available before any ECM is spent.
  • Height bounds for the decomposition (section 7). Replace the scaled bounds by bounds computed from the eta values for the eta quotients and Atkin functions, check the factor used for the trace in the sign-free variant, and halve the plain bound in the ramified case.
  • Root-finding above degree 4096. fproot's parallel multiplication keeps 40 to 65 cores busy for a gain of 1.1 to 3; the parallel first step could be extended to those degrees, or the multiplication fixed. The paper's second algorithm is not needed for space at these sizes.
  • Tighten the model. Measure π and the cost per curve at B1 = 107 and 3·107, which the 1030-bit policy uses and which are extrapolated here; find where the 15–25% optimism comes from (the run counts at the deep sweeps are the place to look); extend it below 640 bits, where the rough-cofactor approximation stops being valid, for the lower levels.
  • For the paper. The model is a heuristic analysis of the search in closed form (the cost per winner is the fixed point of the stopping problem), with a direct experimental check.
